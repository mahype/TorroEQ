# Late-1980s/1990s Hi-Fi Segmented Spectrum-Meter Principles

## How did segmented bar spacing, color zones, peak-hold dots, decay and attack contribute to readability?

### Takeaway
Period displays made the spectrum legible by discretizing both axes: one stable column per filter band and visibly separate level increments. Fast rise, slower fall, and a detached peak marker let the eye read current energy and recent maximum as different signals; restrained color zoning made exceptional levels conspicuous without coloring every fluctuation. [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, p. 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=4#manual); [Sony SEQ-711 photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/b/b1/Sony_SEQ-711-1993.jpg); [VU and PPM Audio Metering](https://www.sound-au.com/project55.htm)

### Cited Findings
- Sony's 1988 SEQ-333ES is the closest direct precedent: a ten-band graphic equalizer with a real-time analyzer display. Its documented centers are 31.5, 63, 125, 250, 500 Hz, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 kHz, and its product image shows ten narrow stacks of discrete luminous elements in a long black recess. - [Audio Database, Sony SEQ-333ES](https://audio-database.com/SONY-ESPRIT/etc/seq-333es.html)
- The 1995 AudioSource EQ Eleven manual describes ten one-octave-spaced controls and a spectrum analyzer divided into ten LED columns corresponding directly to those controls. It says the display shows the relative signal level in each band and is intended both to help set the equalizer and to show musical frequency content. This is strong period documentation for one-column-per-band rather than a continuous waveform. - [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, p. 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=4#manual)
- The EQ Eleven front panel uses separate left and right ten-column analyzers and labels every frequency below its column. The repeated labels and fixed columns make horizontal position categorical rather than continuously sampled. - [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, p. 1](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html#manual)
- Sony's SEQ-711 (1992-1994) used a black display field with two seven-band, dot-matrix-style channel groups. A period product photograph shows narrow luminous columns separated by dark gutters, a cool cyan/white primary display color, sparse red reference markers, and dim unlit matrix elements. - [Sony SEQ-711 product page and photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/index.php/Sony_SEQ-711); [full-size photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/b/b1/Sony_SEQ-711-1993.jpg)
- Sony's SEQ-411 (1990-1994) likewise placed a twin spectrum analyzer centrally between the left and right equalizer sliders. Its photograph shows discrete horizontal luminous segments, substantial black separation between bands, and a limited cool-light/red-accent palette rather than a rainbow gradient. - [Sony SEQ-411 product page and photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/index.php/Sony_SEQ-411); [full-size photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/6/64/Sony_SEQ-411.jpg)
- A conventional logarithmic LED meter using the LM3915 lights successive elements 3 dB apart and supports either a continuous bar or a single-dot mode. The documented example assigns the first seven LEDs to green normal operation and the upper three to red overload, making the upper 9 dB a visually distinct warning region. - [Elliott Sound Products, LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm)
- The same LED-meter reference notes that dot mode consumes less current but is less visible than bar mode. This supports using a filled segmented body for instantaneous level and reserving a single dot only for a secondary peak indication. - [Elliott Sound Products, LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm)
- The metering reference describes LED arrays that combine a bar with one apparently "stuck" LED at a higher level to indicate peak. It also explains why fast peak indication and slower return are useful: a PPM integrates over about 5 ms and falls by 20 dB in about 1.5 seconds, whereas a VU meter takes about 300 ms to settle and therefore represents average level rather than transient peaks. - [Elliott Sound Products, VU and PPM Audio Metering](https://www.sound-au.com/project55.htm)
- IEC TR 60268-18:1995 specifically covers digital peak-level indication for sampled and quantized audio in both professional and consumer use. The public IEC page confirms scope and date but does not expose the standard's detailed ballistics, so exact timing should not be attributed to IEC from this page alone. - [IEC TR 60268-18:1995](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/1217)

### Inferences
- **General design inference:** Make every band a two-cell-wide segmented column with one completely blank cell between columns. The 2:1 luminous-width-to-gutter ratio echoes the dense but clearly separated Sony matrices and prevents adjacent bands from merging into a waveform.
- **General design inference:** Use `▄▄` for each active terminal segment. The dark upper half of the cell supplies a built-in vertical gap, so successive rows read as separate phosphor/LED elements without spending an extra terminal row. Keep inactive cells blank by default; a field of dim `░` cells looks like generic “digital rain” and reduces figure/ground contrast.
- **General design inference:** Render the live level as a contiguous stack from the baseline. Render peak hold as exactly one detached `••` or `▔▔` marker at the peak row, never as a second filled bar. Suppress the marker when it would touch the live bar; otherwise it ceases to communicate “recent maximum.”
- **General design inference:** Use asymmetric ballistics per band: effectively immediate attack (one render frame, or roughly 20-50 ms), 400-700 ms to fall 20 dB for the live bar, a 500-800 ms stationary peak hold, then a slower peak fall of roughly 10-15 dB/s. These are deliberately livelier than broadcast PPM return and should be treated as a readable entertainment-display preset, not a Sony or IEC specification.
- **General design inference:** Apply smoothing in dB space after band-energy calculation, with independent attack and release coefficients. Linear-amplitude smoothing makes quiet movement vanish and loud movement dominate; dB-domain motion better matches equal-height dB segments.
- **General design inference:** Quantize only after smoothing and add about 0.5 segment of hysteresis at each threshold. This prevents rapid one-cell chatter while retaining quick upward response.
- **General design inference:** Keep the body one cool VFD-like hue (cyan, blue-white, or desaturated green). Reserve amber/red for the top one or two rows and peak markers that enter that zone. A frequency-based rainbow wrongly implies that color identifies bass/mid/treble categories; the period Sony examples use color mainly as hierarchy and warning.
- **General design inference:** At silence, clear live bars quickly enough to avoid frozen clutter, but let existing peak markers finish their hold/fall cycle. Do not inject idle animation; the equipment aesthetic depends on the display behaving as an instrument.

### Gaps
- The reviewed Sony user-facing sources and photographs do not publish SEQ-411/SEQ-711 attack, release, refresh rate, or peak-hold timing. The proposed motion constants are therefore implementation guidance, not recovered Sony specifications.
- Still photographs establish segmentation, spacing, palette, and labeling but cannot prove motion behavior. Video demonstrations exist, but their frame rate, camera exposure, unknown repairs, and playback speed make them weak timing references.
- The reviewed Sony displays show red reference/limit accents, but the photographs do not establish a universal Sony color-zone rule. Exact zone boundaries should be validated on the target terminal theme.

## What scales and dynamic ranges were typical or useful?

### Takeaway
A logarithmic dB scale is the defensible default. Ten 3 dB steps (30 dB total) are historically plausible for compact LED hardware; a 12- or 16-row terminal can extend that principle without losing the stepped-meter character. [Elliott Sound Products, LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm)

### Cited Findings
- The Sony SEQ-333ES directly documents the period ten-band sequence `31.5, 63, 125, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k, 8k, 16k`; this is effectively the requested octave layout, with rounded display labels `31` and `62` replacing 31.5 and 63. Its +/-12 dB figure is the equalizer's variable range, not a documented analyzer range. - [Audio Database, Sony SEQ-333ES](https://audio-database.com/SONY-ESPRIT/etc/seq-333es.html)
- A common LM3915-style logarithmic display uses 3 dB between adjacent LEDs, giving 30 dB across ten elements. The cited design explicitly uses one such meter circuit for each frequency band of an analyzer. - [Elliott Sound Products, LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm)
- In the same ten-element example, seven normal LEDs plus three overload LEDs yield a 9 dB overload region. This is evidence for a small upper warning zone, not for coloring half of the scale as "hot." - [Elliott Sound Products, LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm)
- The EQ Eleven's analyzer uses the same ten one-octave centers as its controls: 30, 60, 120, 240, 500 Hz, then 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 kHz. Its sliders provide +/-12 dB adjustment, but the manual calls the analyzer readings "relative signal levels"; the equalizer's +/-12 dB control range should therefore not be mistaken for the analyzer's meter range. - [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, pp. 2 and 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=2#manual); [p. 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=4#manual)
- Sony's SEQ-711 used seven approximately octave-spaced preferred centers: 63, 160, 400 Hz, 1, 2.5, 6.3, and 16 kHz, with a +/-10 dB equalizer range. This corroborates the period practice of stable, logarithmically spaced center-frequency categories even though that Sony model was seven-band rather than ten-band. - [Sony SEQ-711 specifications](https://hifi-wiki.com/index.php/Sony_SEQ-711)
- A standard PPM's cited response is 1 dB below steady-state for a 10 ms burst, 2 dB below for 5 ms, and 4 dB below for 3 ms; its decay reaches -20 dB in 1.5 seconds. These values show that a useful visual meter intentionally filters extremely short events and returns much more slowly than it rises. - [Elliott Sound Products, VU and PPM Audio Metering](https://www.sound-au.com/project55.htm)
- A VU meter is specified to reach 99% in 300 ms and is optimized toward average/perceived level rather than transient peak. This is too slow as the sole behavior for an animated spectrum body, but it is a useful upper bound for "calm" averaging. - [Elliott Sound Products, VU and PPM Audio Metering](https://www.sound-au.com/project55.htm)

### Inferences
- **General design inference: recommended default scale:** use 12 visible rows at 3 dB per row, for a 36 dB window. Label only `0`, `-12`, `-24`, and `-36 dB` at the left edge. This retains the historically common 3 dB cadence while giving music enough room to breathe.
- **General design inference: tall layout:** if 16 rows are reliably available, use 2 dB per row for a 32 dB window. The finer quantization makes moderate changes easier to see, but labels should remain sparse (`0`, `-8`, `-16`, `-24`, `-32`) so the panel does not become a laboratory plot.
- **General design inference: compact layout:** for 8-10 rows, keep 3 dB per row (24-30 dB). Do not stretch a 60- or 90-dB audio range into ten cells; most musical motion would collapse into the bottom rows.
- **General design inference:** define the displayed top as a calibrated reference, preferably `0 dB` relative to the analyzer's chosen full-scale/reference level, not automatically digital clipping. Put the normal/hot boundary 6 dB below the top: cool color below `-6 dB`, amber at `-6` and `-3 dB`, red only at `0 dB` or on actual over-reference events.
- **General design inference:** use a slow global auto-range only if input level is uncontrolled. Move the reference by no more than about 3 dB/s and never independently normalize each band, because per-band normalization destroys cross-band comparison. A fixed reference is preferable when the audio pipeline supplies calibrated dBFS values.
- **General design inference:** compute each displayed value as band RMS or short-window power converted to dB; use a separate faster peak detector only for the hold marker. A maximum FFT bin per band would make wide high-frequency bands appear artificially stronger and noisier.
- **General design inference:** preserve the exact requested centers and labels in this order: `31  62  125  250  500  1k  2k  4k  8k  16k`. This is the clearest compact-label translation of the SEQ-333ES sequence and satisfies the product's fixed contract.

### Gaps
- The public manuals reviewed do not specify the analyzer's own vertical dB calibration or noise floor. Product control ranges of +/-10 or +/-12 dB are documented, but they are not evidence of meter dynamic range.
- “Typical” consumer analyzer timing and range varied by display driver, detector circuit, and marketing intent. A single industry-wide consumer spectrum-analyzer standard was not found.
- IEC TR 60268-18 is paywalled beyond its public scope statement, so it was not used here to claim detailed digital-meter step sizes or timing constants.

## How can these principles translate into Unicode/terminal cells without looking generic or noisy?

### Takeaway
Treat the terminal as a low-resolution segmented VFD, not as a plotting canvas: ten invariant columns, equal dB rows, black gutters, sparse labels, one hue plus a warning accent, and temporally stable quantization. Identity should come from disciplined geometry and motion rather than borders, gradients, decorative glyphs, or excessive annotations. [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, p. 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=4#manual); [Sony SEQ-711 photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/b/b1/Sony_SEQ-711-1993.jpg)

### Cited Findings
- The 1988 Sony SEQ-333ES provides a direct visual and structural model for the target: ten fixed analyzer columns, tiny center-frequency labels, segmented vertical stacks, and a black display field rather than a connected outline or filled waveform. - [Audio Database, Sony SEQ-333ES](https://audio-database.com/SONY-ESPRIT/etc/seq-333es.html)
- Period documentation explicitly maps each analyzer column to one equalizer control and describes the display as relative levels in those bands. A terminal implementation should preserve this one-to-one mapping and should not interpolate a continuous curve between centers. - [AudioSource EQ Eleven manual, p. 4](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/450054/Audiosource-Eq-11.html?page=4#manual)
- The Sony SEQ-711 photograph shows the relevant visual grammar in a constrained display: black field, fixed frequency labels, repeated luminous matrix elements, dark inter-column space, and very few accent colors. - [Sony SEQ-711 full-size photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/b/b1/Sony_SEQ-711-1993.jpg)
- The Sony SEQ-411 photograph shows that the analyzer remains a compact central instrument rather than filling the whole fascia; controls and analyzer are visually separate. - [Sony SEQ-411 full-size photograph](https://hifi-wiki.com/images/6/64/Sony_SEQ-411.jpg)
- Bar mode is more visible than single-dot mode in the LED-meter reference, while one held element above a bar is an established way to distinguish recent peak from present level. - [LED Audio VU Meter](https://www.sound-au.com/project60.htm); [VU and PPM Audio Metering](https://www.sound-au.com/project55.htm)

### Inferences
- **General design inference: canonical geometry:** allocate 29 cells for the data area: ten bands x two cells plus nine one-cell gutters. Add 5-7 cells at left only if scale labels are shown. Do not draw vertical grid lines; the gutters are the grid.
- **General design inference: canonical glyph set:** active segment `▄▄`; held peak `▔▔` (or `••` if overline alignment is poor); inactive segment two spaces. Use plain ASCII for labels. Test glyph width at startup and fall back to `==`/`--` if the terminal mishandles block elements.
- **General design inference: partial levels:** avoid the eight-step Unicode block ramp inside every row. A segment meter should switch whole segments; fractional blocks reintroduce a continuous waveform texture and shimmer. Use temporal hysteresis, not extra glyph granularity.
- **General design inference: row drawing:** paint from top to bottom in one buffered frame, with each row containing only ten two-cell segment slots. Never clear and redraw columns independently; partial updates can create tearing that reads as false transients.
- **General design inference: labels:** use exactly two label rows if space permits: `31 62 125 250 500 1k 2k 4k 8k 16k`, aligned to column centers, plus an optional small `Hz`/`kHz` cue. Do not repeat units on every band.
- **General design inference: palette:** background black/default; live body cool cyan or blue-white; hot-zone rows amber; over-reference/clip and hot peak marker red. In monochrome, encode the same hierarchy by shape: `▄▄` live, `▔▔` peak, and inverse/bold only for over-reference.
- **General design inference: restraint rules:** no enclosing box unless required by the surrounding UI; no per-band color; no glow simulation with adjacent dim characters; no mirrored reflection; no scrolling history; no random idle motion; no numeric value above every bar. Each adds texture without adding a distinct reading task.
- **General design inference: update cadence:** render at 20-30 frames/s, but drive motion from elapsed time rather than frame counts. This is fast enough for a one-frame attack while limiting terminal traffic and preserving discrete, hardware-like movement.
- **General design inference: stereo:** if the product needs one display, combine channels before band metering using power-consistent summation or the higher channel value. Do not squeeze two bars into each two-cell band. If stereo comparison is essential, use two complete stacked ten-band panes, following the separate left/right organization seen in period equipment.
- **General design inference: concrete 12-row preset:** map rows top-to-bottom to `0, -3, -6, ... -33 dB`; body color cyan through `-9 dB`, amber at `-6` and `-3 dB`, red at `0 dB`; attack 30 ms; live release 500 ms per 20 dB; peak hold 650 ms; peak release 12 dB/s; quantization hysteresis 1.5 dB. This is a starting design to tune with real music and terminal themes, not a historical Sony specification.
- **General design inference: acceptance tests:** verify that a steady pink-noise input appears broadly level across octave bands; an octave-band tone lights one dominant column with sensible adjacent leakage; a click produces a brief body rise plus a readable held marker; silence clears without flicker; and dense music still exposes the broad bass/mid/treble balance at a glance.

### Gaps
- Unicode rendering differs by font, terminal, line spacing, color profile, and whether ambiguous-width characters are treated as one or two cells. The proposed `▄▄`/`▔▔` pair needs validation in every supported terminal.
- The source record supports the visual grammar but does not determine whether cyan, blue-white, green, or amber best matches the application's existing theme. Contrast and color-blind checks remain implementation tests.
- No usability study was found comparing segmented terminal spectrum meters. The terminal recommendations are explicit general design inferences from period hardware, metering behavior, and cell constraints rather than experimentally validated universal rules.
